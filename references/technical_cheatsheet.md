# Technical Analysis Cheat Sheet

Use this when the user asks about chart setups, entries/exits, levels, or any "what does the chart say" question. The goal is to read the actual chart in front of you (or the data the user pasted), not to recite generic TA theory.

## Read the chart in this order

1. **Trend** — Is price above or below the 20, 50, and 200-day moving averages? Are those MAs sloping up, flat, or down? A stock above a rising 200-day in a sea of higher highs and higher lows is in an uptrend; the inverse is a downtrend; everything else is chop.

2. **Structure** — Where are the recent swing highs and swing lows? Draw the line. The most recent swing high is your near-term resistance; the most recent swing low is your near-term support. Don't use round numbers unless price has actually reacted to them.

3. **Volume** — Is the recent move on rising volume (conviction) or fading volume (exhaustion)? A breakout on weak volume is suspect. A breakdown on heavy volume is real.

4. **Momentum** — RSI, MACD. Don't list both unless they're saying different things. RSI > 70 = overbought (in an uptrend, this can stay overbought for a while — don't reflexively call a top), RSI < 30 = oversold. Look for divergences (price making new high, RSI not).

## Common patterns worth naming

Only call a pattern if it's actually there. Don't force it.

- **Breakout** — Price closing above a well-defined resistance level, ideally on above-average volume. The previous resistance often becomes support on a retest.
- **Breakdown** — Inverse. The level breaks, volume confirms, the level often becomes resistance on a bounce.
- **Higher highs / higher lows** — The signature of an uptrend.
- **Lower highs / lower lows** — Downtrend.
- **Range** — Bouncing between two horizontal levels. Trade the edges, not the middle.
- **Pullback to MA** — In an uptrend, price pulling back to the 20- or 50-day and holding is a continuation setup.
- **Failed breakout** — Price pokes above resistance, can't hold, falls back inside the range. Often a reversal signal.

Avoid being precious about exotic patterns (head-and-shoulders, cup-and-handle, etc.) — only invoke them if they're textbook clear, and even then, treat them as evidence not destiny.

## Levels to identify

For any chart, try to give the user concrete numbers:

- **Current price**
- **Nearest support** (recent swing low or moving average being respected)
- **Nearest resistance** (recent swing high or prior breakdown level)
- **Invalidation level** — the level where, if it breaks, the setup is wrong. Be specific. "If price closes below $X on volume, the bullish setup is invalidated."

## Entries, stops, targets

When the user asks about a trade entry:

- **Entry zone** — A range, not a point. "Buy zone $X–$Y, ideally on a pullback to support."
- **Stop level** — Below structure, not at a round number. Where would the thesis be wrong? Place the stop just beyond that.
- **Targets** — Plural. The next resistance level is target 1; if it breaks, the next one is target 2. Don't pretend you know the final destination.
- **Risk/reward** — (target − entry) / (entry − stop). Don't take trades where R:R is worse than ~1.5–2:1 unless you have very high conviction or a small position.

## Reading screenshots

If the user uploads a chart screenshot:

1. Identify ticker and timeframe (daily? hourly? 5-min?). If unclear, ask or state your assumption.
2. Note visible indicators (which MAs, which oscillators).
3. Read off the levels you can see — current price, recent highs/lows, where MAs sit.
4. Be honest about what you can and can't read from the image.

## What technicals don't tell you

Charts describe what has happened. They don't know about earnings tomorrow, an FDA decision next week, or a Fed meeting. Always sanity-check a technical setup against the catalyst calendar before suggesting an entry. A clean breakout the day before earnings is not a clean breakout — it's an event-risk trade dressed up as a chart trade.
